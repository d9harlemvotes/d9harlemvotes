Nonpartisan Voter Education

Why Harlem Matters

D9 Harlem Votes® is a nonpartisan civic-engagement organization. We do not endorse candidates or political parties, distribute campaign materials, or provide candidate lists.

We help Harlem voters understand what offices are on the ballot, what those offices do, and how they affect everyday life.

Why are the June 2026 figures Democratic primary figures?

For the June 23, 2026 primary, the NYC Board of Elections reported contested Democratic Assembly primaries in Assembly Districts 68, 69, 70, and 71. There were no contested Republican Assembly primaries on the ballot in these four districts, so there are no comparable Republican turnout figures for those races. The figures shown reflect the contests reported by the NYC Board of Elections, not a decision by D9 Harlem Votes® to favor one political party.

How Harlem Compares

The same percentage can look different when we add context.

The comparison below places Community Districts 9, 10, and 11 alongside selected Manhattan communities. These are geographic comparisons. The districts are not racially uniform.

A participation gap is visible.

In the 2025 primary, turnout across Community Districts 9, 10, and 11 ranged from 28.2% to 34.9%. In the selected Manhattan comparison communities, turnout ranged from 41.1% to 51.2%. The numbers show a participation gap in this comparison. They do not, by themselves, tell us why that gap exists.

Civic Participation Is More Than Voting

The Census and redistricting shape representation.

The Census counts the people living in our communities. Those numbers are used for representation, redistricting, planning, and federal funding formulas that affect programs and services.

After the Census, district boundaries may change as populations shift. In New York City, public hearings are part of the City Council redistricting process.

  • Being counted affects representation.
  • District lines affect who represents a community.
  • Residents can participate in public redistricting hearings.

What We Are Hearing

The numbers give us one part of the story.

The data does not tell us everything about why people vote or why they do not.

Through meaningful engagement, community feedback, and ongoing conversations with Harlem residents, we are developing a fuller picture of what the data may or may not be showing and why.

We use what we learn to strengthen our voter education, civic engagement messaging, and outreach strategies.

Sources and Data Notes

June 23, 2026 primary: NYC Board of Elections certified primary results and New York State Board of Elections active party enrollment. NYC BOE uses the term Total Applicable Ballots for ballots eligible to count in the specific Assembly contest.

Census and redistricting: U.S. Census Bureau and NYC Districting Commission.

Election records do not identify individual voters by race. Geographic turnout data can show participation across Harlem communities, but it cannot tell us how individual Black voters cast their ballots or which candidates they supported.
